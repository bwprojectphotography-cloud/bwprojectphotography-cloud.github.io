Nikon Zf: First Impressions

This review isn't finished yet. These are all my impressions so far from actually using the camera — I'll keep adding to it as I shoot more.

01Looks

It LOOKS good. It's a really good-looking camera, and that surprised me, since I've always been into rangefinder-style cameras. But I like the presence it has — retro, but sleek and modern somehow. It really does look like a film camera, and I really enjoy carrying it around. And it looks damn good sitting on the coffee table.

02Handling

The grip is uncomfortable — this is definitely a camera you want a grip or a strap for. It's chunky, but not overly heavy. Now that I have a strap, it's a lot more handleable, since the weight shifts to my neck.

The dials feel great. They're very clicky. They might be a little heavy to click, but I think that's a good thing — on other cameras I've had with dials like these, they tend to accidentally move around.

I really enjoy the black and white dial. I find myself shooting black and white more often.

03Shutter

At first, I didn't like the shutter. One of the main reasons I didn't like the S9 was the lack of a mechanical shutter, and with the size and look of this camera, you'd expect a clack — a nice, heavy shutter, since it looks like an old-school Nikon. Instead it's soft and light, not satisfying at all.

But after more time shooting with this camera, the shutter sound started growing on me. It's still not a satisfying mechanical shutter like on a film camera, but it has its own quality to it — and it's nice for more intimate spaces.

04Color & image quality

The more I use it, the more I enjoy the color science. I started messing with photo recipes from serbanjpg.com — I think. I'm really enjoying the colors, and the JPEGs look very nice and clean. Lately I've been shooting the Modern Chrome recipe, just JPEGs. I think a camera should be able to take great images without needing to edit them.

Honestly, as I've used more cameras, I realize color science isn't as big of a thing anymore. You can pretty much edit anything into anything else. But having a system that lets you add recipes or LUTs really makes shooting more fun, because you can just focus on shooting — not so much on the edit. Don't get me wrong, sometimes editing can be fun. But it's also nice to focus purely on the photographing part.

After nearly 10 years shooting with modern cameras, it's almost impossible to get a truly bad image out of one. Nowadays, if you're good at what you do, you can get good images out of anything. That being said — this Nikon shoots great images.

05Out in the world

The camera is not as discreet as other cameras I've used — it grabs attention. That being said, it's still not DSLR-in-someone's-face level. I think that's largely because it looks like a film camera.

06The 40mm f/2

I purchased a 40mm f/2 — it seems to be one of the more budget options. It's plastic, and it's really light, and pancake-style, so it's very sleek. It's definitely not the sharpest lens I've ever used, and its character isn't extremely interesting, but it's sharp enough. I think the 40mm focal length is very natural. I used to be a big 35mm shooter, but I ended up getting bored of that focal length — it's always too wide when you want to be closer, and too tight when you want it to be wider. 40mm is a really nice walking-around lens. It just seems to match what I see with my own eyes, and it tends to lean tighter. Overall the image quality is nice, the bokeh is really enjoyable, and the contrast on the lens is quite nice.

07The Software (and the Recipe Problem)

Real quick, I want to mention the software. This is something that doesn't really come up a lot on cameras, especially when you're mostly a dedicated photographer. Once you get your settings dialed in, it's not something you're moving around too often — at least I'm not.

That being said, the Nikon software is fairly straightforward. Now that I've used all the systems, I think Sony continues to be the most complex one in terms of menus and submenus. Nikon is not much better, but it's not the worst. I would say overall Fujifilm has the best settings layout, in my opinion — but maybe that's just because I used it for so much longer.

One thing I will say is the worst out of any camera brand: the way you connect your camera to your phone is absolutely ridiculous.

Part of the big sell on the Nikon Zf is the ability to use these photo recipes, or film simulations, whatever you want to call them. Lumix has an amazing system where you can just upload LUTs — the same ones you use while you edit photos or videos — and it's absolutely fantastic. You connect your camera to your phone and just upload them. Pretty straightforward. Fujifilm, you do everything in camera: you mess with the settings, you set it to the right specifications, and you go from there. I don't believe Sony or Canon have something similar. When it comes to Nikon, it is the weirdest setup I've seen so far.

Not only do they not have an app you can use to download them to your camera, like Lumix — you can't just dial them in camera either. They have to be uploaded onto a website, which then connects to your camera's cloud. And there's no button or anything to send it directly to your camera. You just have to wait until your camera syncs with the cloud and downloads it. And maybe I'm just not smart enough, but I genuinely cannot see a better way — you literally upload it to the cloud, then turn your camera on, connect to the cloud, and just wait until it decides it's time to download.

It is by far one of the worst ways to get something like this working. I think if they made either a separate app, or integrated it into the one app Nikon does have, and just let you browse recipes or download recipes and manually upload them, that would be a game changer. Whatever this is would make a lot of people shy away from it — especially people who are not used to dealing with tech or troubleshooting. I'm honestly surprised this is Nikon's current offering, and I'm hoping they figure something out. Then again, I don't think this photo recipe feature is compatible with a lot of their stuff.

08Bonus thoughts

The only thing I'm still figuring out is how to properly meter this sensor. Some sensors are really fine with being overexposed — I've mainly seen this with Fujifilm cameras. I just have not dialed in my settings yet.

Albany Park: A Photo Book

I'm making a photo book about Albany Park.

"It's not that bad here. I would live here."

I've lived in Chicago for going on seven years now, almost all of it on the North Side. Before that, the northwest suburbs — Glenview, Northbrook. But I grew up around people who looked like me: Mexicans and Latinos, families that, in many ways, looked like mine.

As a kid, I had a strange relationship with being Mexican. My family put a huge value on owning your own business and working hard — almost every aunt and uncle I knew either owned one or was in the process of starting one. But I looked at the Mexican kids around me and judged them. I decided I was different. So I ran from my own culture: I wouldn't play soccer, I wouldn't speak Spanish, I wouldn't associate with the Mexican kids. I didn't realize I was separating myself from the only community I had.

Eventually I moved into affluent, mostly white neighborhoods — and realized I wasn't one of them either. To them, I was still just another Mexican kid. Strangely, that realization helped me find myself. Little by little, I stopped being ashamed of where I came from. But by the time I figured that out, I'd already lost part of my connection. Spanish was my first language, and I'd spent years barely speaking it.

I've spent almost a decade trying to realign myself with where I come from.

Last year, I almost had a child. That brought the question back: what would I pass down? What sense of community could I give them?

Then I started spending time in Albany Park. A friend of mine lived here, a Mexican girl, and every time I visited I felt something I couldn't quite explain — something familiar. Something that felt like home. When I walk through Lakeview or Lincoln Park, I can have fun, but sometimes I still feel like I'm visiting. In Albany Park, I see myself. I hear Spanish. I see businesses owned by people who remind me of my family. I see pieces of my mother and father.

So I moved here. And I've been walking around with my camera ever since.

The book is about the beauty of ordinary life here. A storefront. A restaurant. A family walking down the street. A handwritten sign. A kid riding a bike. None of these things are extraordinary on their own. But together, they create something. A feeling. A sense of belonging.

I'm not trying to convince anyone Albany Park is perfect. It isn't. I'm simply trying to show you what I see when I walk through it.

For a long time, I thought becoming someone meant separating myself from where I came from. Now I'm starting to think becoming yourself might mean finding your way back.

Street Photography in 2026: Is It Unethical Now?

I've always had an internal struggle over what version is the right or correct approach.

This is a topic that has been on my mind for the last few years. When I started photography, I was almost immediately drawn to street photography. Possibly because it had the least barrier to entry. Possibly because I loved the raw truth of the format — the documentation of it, the straightforward approach to it. Unlike portrait work, where you do heavy editing to make a stylized version of reality and the subject, street photography is honest. You get what you see. Regardless of why, it seems to be my chosen art form. It's a way to document not only the life I see as an individual, but also the life that is.

That being said, there is some ethical discourse I've seen over the years I've spent doing this form of photography. There seem to be groups of people that go around asking strangers if they can take their image. That seems to be a very connecting version of street photography — creating relationships with people while still capturing essentially strangers. Then I've seen people that shoot far and discreet, capturing filmic, movie-like scenes of the city or the location. Sometimes I like falling into this category. Then there is a final group that takes a very intense, almost invasive approach. It's in your face, no questions or consent asked — just a photographer choosing a moment and capturing the reality of that moment.

I rarely fall in the first category, as I do believe that kind of eliminates the realness of a moment. Most often I choose to be a silent observer, far away, capturing the beauty of a moment. But other times I feel like the truth of the moment supersedes the momentary discomfort of everyone.

Recently I had a situation that got intense, with someone following me into a building after taking photos — even though that person was not the subject. To be honest, I was not even aware of them at the moment. It got me thinking about how people react to cameras, and how it's changed over just the recent years that I've done this art form.

Although it's always caught people off guard, and sometimes led to some uncomfortable confrontations, I feel like as technology becomes ever-present in our lives, people are much more guarded and hypervigilant of cameras. I have noticed a growing discomfort with the camera even being present. Lately in the news we've been seeing discourse around Flock cameras and other social monitoring. Now everyone lives almost a split — or maybe a layered life would be a better way to describe it. One part of our existence is in the real, natural world, and the second is as a presence in a digital layer that now exists at all times. It feels like people are now so hypervigilant about that second layer being affected. People are scared to show up online and randomly have a post be about them — to find themselves on the other side of a TikTok or a post.

So that brings me to think. In the early years of this art form, film photographers would do street photography and develop, and at best have images that no one would ever see but themselves — or at worst, a book was made that only a select community might even be interested in. Now a street photographer's post, video content, can go live into a world where millions of people can stumble upon it. Is it not morally less ethical? Is it now a version of art that can be deemed unethical — or one of those things that will be remembered as a morally wrong part of a time, the way certain words used to describe people have become outdated and incorrect?

About

Kevin Camacho

Street photographer in Albany Park, Chicago. Chasing light, building toward a photo book.

I've been shooting for about ten years.

It started earlier than that, really. Ever since I was a child I've had this urge to capture what I saw — so I started by drawing and painting. That was my main thing. I've been an artist as long as I can remember; since I was five years old I knew I liked making things.

Photography gave me the ability of documentation I'd always wanted. I used to picture little frames of my life and save them away in my memory. I begged for a camera pretty much my entire childhood. I never got one — so I bought one for myself when I got older.

I shoot street photography and urban landscapes. I'm not very good at pretty much anything else, but I love carrying my camera around and documenting life as it goes. Street photography has a special pull for me — it's about capturing the moment I live in, so I can see it in the future when time has changed, life has changed, everything looks different. I want to cement what I saw.

My goal is simple — make things I like, and sometimes post them so other people can see them.

Chicago has been my muse for almost the entire ten years. I want to start traveling to more cities, but there's something special about Chicago. Right now I'm working on a book about Albany Park.

I'm just here documenting what I feel.
